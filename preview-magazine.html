Editor's NoteWandering Gorilla · May 1956
From · The · Editor

A short note, before you begin.

On the way the journal is put together, what it is for, and a thank-you to the small list of people who keep it going.

This issue is the seventh of the journal’s third year, and the first that I have laid out from a stationary van. The previous six were assembled on the road, sometimes on the dashboard, twice in the laundromat in Tonopah, once on a friend’s kitchen table in West Virginia at three in the morning. There is, I am told, a certain reliability that comes from settling in one place. I have not yet acquired it, but the work has.

Each issue contains six departments: Dispatch, the short letter from wherever the van currently is; The Workshop, four reports from the small ongoing tool-building practice that runs alongside everything else; The Field, a short photo essay and a longer piece of writing; Equipment, an updated inventory of the rig; The Route, a hand-drawn map of where the van has been; and Subscriptions, in the back, the means by which letters arrive and depart.

— J., Bend, Oregon · 8 May 1956

Dept. 01 · DispatchFiled 8 May 1956
Department · One Dispatch from the Road

Three weeks in one place.

A correspondent’s short report from behind a friend’s shop in Bend, Oregon — what is being read, written, and slowly cooked at the back of the van.

The road, like the work, has been quiet. I’ve been parked behind a friend’s machine shop on the east side of Bend for nineteen days now, the longest stretch in one place since I started, and the days have taken on the slow shape of a town visit rather than a stop. The mornings begin with a bad espresso at the coffee shop on Wall Street, where the machine is older than the barista and where the Three Sisters are visible above the rooftops on the days the smoke isn’t bad.

The afternoons are for the desk: a fold-down arm against the side of the van, a 27-inch monitor I have learned to live with despite its terrible viewing angles, and a notebook that has now made it to its third state in eight months. Most of the work, this month, is on a piece of code I have been rewriting since Gunnison, Colorado, where I was forced to confront, in a gravel parking lot with no service for four hours, that the cache I had written in 2024 was simply wrong.

9
Dispatch · continuedDept. 01

The evenings have been social, which is a rare thing. There is a small group of writers and welders here who have been kind enough to invite me to their porch on Tuesdays, where we eat indifferent pizza and argue, gently, about the right number of axles for a serious truck. I am leaving them next week, with reluctance, for a stretch of solitude in the Alvord, but the porch will hold. It has, before.

The weather has been bright and cold. I keep the side door of the van open while I work, against the advice of everyone who has ever met me, because I cannot stand to be enclosed when there is high desert outside it. On the days I cannot stand the desk either, I drive forty minutes to the lava-tube caves at Lava River and walk in the dark for an hour. I do not recommend it, exactly. I do recommend it.

“The right time to rewrite a piece of code is the morning you understand, in your body, why it was wrong.”

Next week the van goes south to the Alvord Desert for three nights of nothing — no service, no porch, no espresso. The cache rewrite will be finished by then, or it will not. The map application that depends on it will ship to the seventeen people on the list when both are satisfied.

Department · Two The Workshop

Four projects, none finished.

Status reports from the small portable workshop at the back of the van — what is being built, what has stalled, and what is shipping in spite of itself.

Plate IThe map, with no service at all.

Detour · An offline map

P-014 · In Progress · Day 47

An offline-first map for routes the FCC has forgotten about: vector tiles, hand-clipped regions, a small persistence layer that makes no assumptions about disks. The current version is a rewrite of one I shipped in 2024, which assumed disks were fast and which I was forced to apologise for, in person, to a friend who watched it crawl in the Wind River Range.

The piece I am proudest of is small: a twenty-line heuristic that decides which tiles to keep when storage is short. It runs in the background while the van moves and feels, when it works, like the attentive part of the brain that decides which book to bring on a trip.

Plate IITwo thousand slides, none catalogued.

Slide · A film archive

P-013 · Paused · 62 Days

A slow, color-faithful photo library, built around Kodachrome scans that are more or less the reason this journal exists. The interface is closer to a contact sheet than to a feed. The colour pipeline assumes the photographs were made on emulsions nobody is making anymore.

Paused, with apologies, since March, on account of a calibrated monitor I keep almost ordering and then talking myself out of, on the grounds that monitors are large and the van is small. It will be a winter project again.

Plate IIINotes that live next to the books.

Marginalia · Notes that stay home

P-012 · Shipping · v0.4

A note-taking tool that lives next to your books and not inside your phone. Plain text on disk, syncs over the local network, ignores the cloud entirely. The CRDT layer — source of four months of mild misery — is finally calm enough to ship. Forty-seven subscribers get the new build first, from a campsite in the Steens, probably without service.

Dept. 02 · The WorkshopContinued
Plate IVThe engine of this journal.

Field Kit · This journal's engine

P-011 · Seed · Public Soon

The static-site engine this journal runs on. Markdown, plus a long opinionated stylesheet, plus one Python script that takes the long view of what a page is. It was pulled out of the rest of this site over the weekend, on the grounds that two friends had asked, separately, whether they could borrow it.

They have, between them, broken it in three interesting ways already — one of which involved a Cyrillic filename and a deeply confused YAML parser. I count that as the first release. A public version will go up at the end of the month, with documentation written, as is the custom, after the fact.

“A workshop in a van is mostly the discipline of putting things back where they came from, on a moving floor.”

Dept. 03 · The FieldAn Essay
Notes · By · The · Editor

Notes on returning to a viewpoint, half a century late.

On a photograph from 1953, the place it described, and the trouble with seeing a thing for the first time when you have already seen it.

The car in the photograph is a 1951 Buick. The mountains have not moved. The light, if you arrive at the right hour in October, is more or less the same light the original photographer had, half a century earlier — which is the part of the story I find most difficult to get used to. So little of what we make outlives us; the place itself is doing nothing of the kind.

I first saw the Tunnel View in a magazine my grandfather kept on the porch, a 1957 issue of Holiday, browning at the edges, the Kodachrome plate still impossibly saturated under the foxing. Half Dome looked, in that picture, like something painted by a very confident amateur. I was eight, I think, and decided, with the particular certainty of an eight-year-old, that I would one day stand on the same wall and see the same thing for myself.

30
An Essay · continuedDept. 03
“The valley does not perform for the visitor. It simply continues, and you are permitted, briefly, to walk through it.”

I did not stand on the same wall for thirty-three years. When I finally did, in October of last year, with a digital camera that flatters everything and a paper map that flatters nothing, I tried to keep the original photograph out of my head. It was useless. The image had set the cliff in place long before I arrived; what I was actually doing, in the parking lot in late afternoon, was checking whether the view still matched the picture.

It did. I sat on the wall for an hour, ate a sandwich, did not take a picture. Took one on the way out anyway. The exposure was wrong; the light had moved while I sat, in the slow imperceptible way that light moves over a granite face, and the cliff that was orange when I arrived was nearly red when I left. I have not printed the frame. It is, as far as I am concerned, still my grandfather’s picture.

What changes, in fifty years, is the photographer and the things on the wall around them. The strangers in the picture from 1953 — the boys leaning on the Buick, the woman in red shorts — are at this point, statistically, gone. The Buick is gone. The pullout has been repaved twice. The view is, as I said, the same view.

I am writing this in the van, in Bend, Oregon, six hours’ drive north and east of that pullout. The grandfather is also gone. The magazine, somehow, is still on the porch.

Dept. 06 · SubscriptionsLetters to the Editor
Department · Six Subscriptions & Letters

If you are nearby, say hello.

A short list of how to write in. Letters are answered, eventually. Postcards are sent in return. The sole subscription rate is patience.

The journal goes out, when it goes out, by post and by e-mail in roughly equal measure. The postal list is older and slower; the e-mail list is newer and includes, increasingly, people I have never met in person but who have written, at some point, a kind paragraph about an entry. Both are read by the editor before sending, which is a polite way of saying that they are short.

If you are within an afternoon’s drive of Bend, Oregon between now and roughly the second week of June, write in advance. The porch on Tuesdays welcomes new arrivals if there is room and warm pizza if there isn’t. After that, the van turns south, and the next reliable stop is the Alvord, where there will be no service for a while.
